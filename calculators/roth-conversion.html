Roth conversion calculator

A Roth conversion moves money from a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. The amount you convert is added to this year's income and taxed. Enter this year's wages and an amount to see where your bracket ends, the federal tax on the conversion, and how that compares with paying tax on the same dollars later.

How this calculator works

Tax on the conversion is your federal income tax with the conversion added, minus your tax without it, using the 2026 brackets and the standard deduction. "Room left in your bracket" is the gap between your taxable income and the top of your current bracket: converting up to that amount keeps every converted dollar at your current rate.

The comparison with later uses the same dollars taxed at the rate you enter. If the tax comes out of the account in both cases, growth multiplies both sides by the same amount, so the comparison holds at any return. Paying the conversion tax from savings favors the Roth a little more. Your future tax rate is a guess, so try more than one.

Assumptions: federal income tax only (your state may tax the conversion too); wages are your only other income; standard deduction; no after-tax (nondeductible) money in any traditional IRA you own. If you have after-tax money in a traditional IRA, the pro-rata rule decides how much of a conversion is taxed and this math doesn't apply. A conversion also raises income used for the marketplace premium tax credit and, from age 63, for Medicare premiums two years later. Those effects are not calculated here.

Sources

  • A conversion is taxable income; conversions can't be recharacterized (undone) since 2018: IRS Publication 590-A.
  • Each conversion has its own 5-year period; the 10% additional tax on early distributions before age 59½: IRS Publication 590-B.
  • 2026 tax brackets and standard deduction: IRS Rev. Proc. 2025-32.
  • Medicare income-related premiums use income from two years earlier: SSA POMS HI 01101.010.
