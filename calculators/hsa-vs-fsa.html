How this calculator works

On the same dollars, an HSA and an FSA save exactly the same tax in year one: both skip federal income tax, and both skip Social Security and Medicare tax when they go through payroll. The differences are what happens to unspent money and how much you can put in.

  • FSA: money you don't spend by the deadline is lost, unless your plan offers a carryover or a grace period (a plan can offer one or the other, not both: IRS Publication 969; Notice 2013-71).
  • HSA: the money is yours to keep and can be invested. You need a qualifying high-deductible health plan.
  • Receipts strategy: one approach some people use is to pay medical costs out of pocket, keep the receipts, and leave the full HSA invested. IRS Publication 969 sets no deadline for reimbursing yourself for qualified costs incurred after the HSA was opened.

Assumptions: federal tax only, standard deduction, W-2 wages, no other income. Payroll tax savings assume wages under the Social Security wage base; above it, only Medicare is saved. Above $200,000 of wages ($250,000 married filing jointly) an extra 0.9% Medicare tax applies (not modeled; IRS Topic 560). California and New Jersey don't follow the federal HSA tax break, so HSA contributions are taxed by those states (California FTB 3805P instructions; New Jersey Assembly Bill A2634 statement, 2026). The return is an assumption, not a promise.
